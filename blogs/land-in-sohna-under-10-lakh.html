Land in Sohna Under 10 Lakh? The Kanal Math Sellers Skip

✍️ By Ritika Sharma 📅 15 October 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read
⚡ Quick Answer

You may find land in Sohna under 10 lakh, but it will usually be a small share of agricultural or village land, not a ready residential plot. Before you get excited, convert the seller's rate into square yards (gaj), check what the land can legally be used for, and confirm your name will appear in the revenue records. Land sold by the kanal, marla or acre often hides the true price per gaj, so do the math yourself and have a lawyer verify the Jamabandi and khasra before paying.

"Land" is a slippery word in Sohna. To one buyer it means a 50 gaj plot in a neat colony. To another it means a few marlas of farmland on the edge of a village. Both get advertised in the same breath, often at the same headline price, and that's where trouble begins.

If you searched for land in Sohna under 10 lakh, there's a good chance you want the cheapest possible way into this belt. That's a reasonable goal. It's also the budget where understanding the paperwork matters most, because the lower the price, the more likely the land is agricultural, shared or restricted.

This guide explains how land is measured and priced here, how to convert a seller's quote into something you can compare, which records to check, and which red flags to take seriously. It draws on the buyer conversations and site visits we handle. It is general information, not legal or financial advice, so have your own lawyer read the documents for any specific parcel.

Land vs Plot: Not the Same Product

A plot usually means a demarcated piece of land in a planned layout, with a plot number, an approved map and, in a licensed colony, a defined purpose, mostly residential. A land parcel can mean almost anything else: agricultural land, village land, a share in a larger holding, or an unplanned stretch beside a road.

The difference shows up in what you can do next.

  • Plots in licensed layouts are generally meant for building, subject to approvals.
  • Agricultural land is meant for farming. Building a home on it usually needs a change of land use and other permissions.
  • Shares in larger parcels may leave you owning an undivided portion that is hard to use, fence or sell.

If your real goal is a place to build, read our companion guide on a plot for sale in Sohna under 10 lakh. This article stays with land in the broader sense.

The Unit Cheat Sheet: Acre, Kanal, Marla and Gaj

Sellers in Haryana quote rural land in acres, kanals and marlas. Plotted layouts quote in gaj, which is the same as a square yard. Until you convert between them, you can't compare a farm offer with a plot offer.

UnitEquals (Square Yards / Gaj)
1 Gaj1 square yard (9 sq ft)
1 MarlaAbout 30.25 gaj
1 Kanal (20 marla)605 gaj
1 Acre (8 kanal)4,840 gaj

Standard Haryana conversions. Confirm the unit and area stated in the actual records for any parcel.

These numbers are the whole secret. A quote that sounds modest per acre may look very different per gaj, and the reverse is true too.

Convert Any Quote in 60 Seconds

Here is the formula we give every buyer:

  1. Ask for the total price and the exact area being sold, in the unit used in the records.
  2. Convert the area into gaj using the table above.
  3. Divide the total price by the number of gaj to get the price per gaj.
  4. Compare that with the per-gaj rates of nearby plotted layouts.

In the documented layouts we've visited recently, 50 gaj plots have been quoted at about ₹18 to 24 lakh, which works out to roughly ₹36,000 to 48,000 per gaj. These are indicative figures. Use them as a yardstick to see whether a land offer is genuinely cheaper or just differently packaged.

An illustrative example

This is a hypothetical, not a market rate. Suppose a seller quotes ₹30 lakh per kanal. With a ₹10 lakh budget and a cushion of roughly 8 to 10 percent for charges, your quoted-price ceiling is about ₹9 to 9.25 lakh.

  • ₹9 lakh divided by ₹30 lakh per kanal is 0.3 kanal, which is 6 marla.
  • 6 marla is about 181 gaj.
  • ₹9 lakh for 181 gaj works out to roughly ₹5,000 per gaj.

That looks cheap next to a plotted layout, and that's exactly the point. A big gap in price per gaj almost always reflects a gap in what you're buying: land use, access, approvals or the right to build. The cheaper number is not a discount. It's a different product.

What You Can Legally Do With the Land

Before price, ask the question that decides the value: what is this land allowed to be used for?

  • Land use and zoning. Land is classified under the area's development plan. Agricultural land cannot simply be built on as residential. Ask your lawyer to check the zoning with the concerned planning department.
  • Change of land use. Converting agricultural land for other uses requires permission and fees, and approval is not guaranteed. A seller saying "it will be converted soon" is not the same as a conversion order in hand.
  • Aravalli and land-preservation rules. Parts of the Aravalli belt around Sohna carry restrictions on construction and land-use change. Ask for written confirmation that the parcel is not covered by such restrictions and check any recent court or government orders affecting the area.
  • Planned roads and acquisition. Confirm the land isn't marked for a road, a public project or acquisition under the development plan.

If the parcel can't be used the way you intend, a low price is the least interesting fact about it.

The Revenue Records That Matter

Agricultural land is tracked in revenue records, and these decide whether the seller can actually sell to you.

  • Jamabandi. The record of rights showing who is recorded as owner and in what share. The seller's name should match, and the area should match what you're being offered.
  • Khasra and khewat numbers. These identify the exact parcel and the holding it belongs to. Insist the sale deed names the same numbers you see on the ground.
  • Girdawari. The field-level record of who cultivates and possesses the land. It helps show who is actually in possession.
  • Mutation (Dakhil Kharij). After a sale, the revenue record must be updated in your name. Registration alone is not the same.
  • Encumbrance and loans. Ask whether the land is mortgaged, attached or under a pending case.

A point many buyers miss: farmland is often held jointly by several family members. If the Jamabandi shows multiple co-owners, every one of them must agree to the sale, or you risk buying a dispute. Also, laws on the fragmentation of agricultural holdings can limit how small a parcel of farmland may be sold, so ask your lawyer whether the portion on offer can legally be carved out and registered. 7 Red Flags Specific to Land Deals

  1. The seller offers an agreement or power of attorney only. These do not, on their own, transfer ownership. A registered sale deed does.
  2. Several co-owners, one signer. Everyone recorded must consent.
  3. "Share" instead of a demarcated parcel. If you can't point to your exact land on the ground, be cautious.
  4. No clear legal access. A parcel with no recorded, motorable approach is hard to use and hard to resell.
  5. The price is far below nearby rates with no clear reason. The gap is usually the risk.
  6. Someone else is in possession. Check who is farming or occupying the land.
  7. Pressure to pay before verification. "Rate goes up Monday" is a sales line, not a fact.

Any single flag isn't proof of fraud, but each is reason enough to slow down.

What to Check on the Ground

Paper tells half the story. A site visit tells the rest.

  • Demarcation. Ask for the boundaries to be marked and compare them with the map. If there is doubt, get the land measured by a qualified surveyor or through the proper revenue process.
  • Access. Walk the route in. Check that the road reaches the parcel and who owns it.
  • Terrain and water. Rocky ground, steep slopes and low spots that collect rainwater change what the land is worth and what you can do with it.
  • Neighbours. A short, polite conversation can reveal boundary disputes or possession claims no document mentions.
  • Utilities. Ask how far electricity and water lines are, and who would bring them.

Visit more than once, and once after rain if you can.

Who This Suits, and Who Should Skip It

It may suit you if you…

  • Want to hold land for the long term and can wait.
  • Are comfortable with the slower process of verification and possible conversion.
  • Have a lawyer to guide you through revenue records.

Consider a plot instead if you…

  • Want to build a home in the near future.
  • Need a bank loan and a clear, approvable property.
  • Prefer a ready, documented parcel over a project to complete.

Two Buyers, Two Outcomes

This is an illustrative scenario, not a real client, but the pattern repeats.

Buyer A sees land advertised "under ₹10 lakh" and gets excited by the per-gaj figure. The seller offers six marla from a larger family holding. A signs an agreement and pays a token. Later the lawyer finds the Jamabandi lists four co-owners, and only one has agreed to sell. Recovering the money becomes a project of its own.

Buyer B asks for the khasra and khewat numbers first, converts the quote into gaj, and has a lawyer read the Jamabandi and zoning before paying. The lawyer flags that the parcel falls in a restricted zone. B walks away, having spent only a modest fee, and later buys a documented plot elsewhere.

The difference was order of operations. B verified first and paid second.

Your Verification Routine

  1. Get the area and total price in writing, in the unit used in the records.
  2. Convert to gaj and compare with nearby plotted rates.
  3. Ask for the full document list: sale deed chain, Jamabandi, khasra and khewat details, mutation, girdawari and encumbrance position.
  4. Check land use, zoning and Aravalli status in writing with your lawyer.
  5. Visit, demarcate and walk the access route.
  6. Confirm every co-owner consents and that the parcel can legally be sold as offered.
  7. Pay only against written receipts, then register, complete mutation and keep certified copies.
